Every line, by how fast it really moves

Speed across every run a service made, short turns included — because on some lines short turns are most of the service. The pale mark is the slowest tenth of runs, so the gap between the two is how much worse a bad trip gets than a normal one.

    Distances are measured straight-line between stations, so they fall a little short of real track length and every speed here reads slightly low. The bias applies to every service equally, so the order holds. The Staten Island Railway is left out: it is a separate railway with stations kilometres apart, and it outruns the whole list without being comparable to any of it.

    Pick a line and two stations. Waiting and riding are reported separately, and deliberately: percentiles do not add, so quoting one combined number would overstate every result. The ride is what the train does. The wait is what the timetable does to you.

    Where it comes from

    The MTA's realtime feeds only look forward: a trip update lists the stops a train has yet to reach and drops each one as it passes, so a finished trip leaves no trace. There is no official archive of where trains actually were. These movements were recorded as they happened by subwaydata.nyc, continuously since 2021. Station names and route order come from the MTA's published schedule.

    What it is not

    A reconstruction, not an official record. Weekdays only. Days with sharply reduced service are detected by trip count and left out of the bands, though you can still draw them. Minutes are local wall-clock, so an 8:15 train is an 8:15 train on both sides of the November clock change.
